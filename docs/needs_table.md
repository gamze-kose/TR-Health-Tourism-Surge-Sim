# Care needs created by crises

The table states what the literature reports for each need, whether it can move abroad, and what share of each care line it takes in the model. Numbers in brackets refer to the list at the end. Statements marked as the authors' judgement have no source and are open to expert review.

## Caused by the crisis

### Major limb injury and reconstruction

Fractures that do not heal, lost soft tissue and damaged nerves need staged reconstruction, often long after the injury.

- Half of the injuries on a WHO register of patients needing medical evacuation from a conflict zone were major limb injuries. [1]
- After the 2015 Nepal earthquake, 66% of injury cases in a tertiary hospital had fractures, and about 76% of fracture cases had surgery. [2]
- In war-wounded civilians treated in a reconstructive surgery programme, 77% of those with bone samples had bone infection, and 55% of infections resisted first-line antibiotics. [3]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. In that programme, patients with bone infection had been injured a median of 19 months earlier (interquartile range 8 to 40). [3] Authors' judgement: Planned reconstruction can take place weeks to years after injury.

**Care needed.** Limb salvage, bone and soft tissue reconstruction, infection control and repeated operations.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Orthopaedic and plastic surgery departments of authorised hospitals.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Intensive care 15%, Surgery and trauma 72%, Rehabilitation 59%; Armed conflict: Intensive care 15%, Surgery and trauma 50%, Rehabilitation 50%.

### Amputation and prosthetic limb

Losing a limb means stump surgery, a prosthesis, training to use it and replacements over the years.

- Amputations were 22% of the injuries on the same WHO register. [1]
- After the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, amputees were 43 of 141 patients (31%) in one subacute rehabilitation unit and 176 of 1,571 (11%, calculated) in a multicentre rehabilitation series. [4], [5]
- One national medical evacuation programme had listed 744 patients for prosthetics, out of 5,621 evacuated abroad. [6]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. Authors' judgement: Once wounds have healed. Prosthetic fitting and training can be planned.

**Care needed.** Stump surgery, prosthetic fitting, gait training and later replacements.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Orthopaedics, physical rehabilitation and prosthetic workshops. No province list of prosthetic workshops was found.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Surgery and trauma 10%, Rehabilitation 21%; Armed conflict: Surgery and trauma 22%, Rehabilitation 22%.

### Spinal cord injury

Spinal cord injury needs months of specialised rehabilitation and lifelong follow-up.

- Spinal cord injuries were 6% of the injuries on the same WHO register. In that setting, the estimated wait for inpatient rehabilitation exceeded 11 months. [1]
- After the 2010 Haiti earthquake, at least 25 to 30 people with spinal cord injury were evacuated abroad. [7]
- Pressure sores were reported in 15 to 56% of spinal cord injury patients in earthquake cohorts from Kashmir, Wenchuan and Haiti. [7]

**Moving abroad.** After stabilising. Authors' judgement: After the spine is stabilised.

**Care needed.** Inpatient rehabilitation, bladder and skin care, wheelchairs and home adaptation.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Physical medicine and rehabilitation hospitals. No source on thermal facilities used after injury was found.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Rehabilitation 8.5%; Armed conflict: Surgery and trauma 6%, Rehabilitation 6%.

### Traumatic brain injury

Severe brain injury needs intensive care first and long neurological rehabilitation after.

- Brain injuries were 5% of the injuries on the same WHO register and 9 of 141 patients (6%) in a rehabilitation unit in Ankara that treated Kahramanmaraş earthquake survivors. [1], [4]
- Neurosurgery, including spine, made up 16% of war-related operations in a ten-year series at a border hospital. [8]

**Moving abroad.** After stabilising. One national evacuation programme moves patients as soon as they are stable enough for transport. [6]

**Care needed.** Neurosurgery, intensive care and neurological rehabilitation.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Neurosurgery and intensive care in authorised hospitals, and rehabilitation hospitals.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Intensive care 5%, Surgery and trauma 1%, Rehabilitation 6.4%; Armed conflict: Intensive care 30%, Surgery and trauma 5%, Rehabilitation 5%.

### Major burns

Major burns need specialised units first, then repeated surgery for scars that limit movement.

- Burns were 4% of the injuries on the same WHO register. [1]
- Türkiye reported 28 burn centres and 48 burn units with 840 beds in 2025. No list by province was found. [9]

**Moving abroad.** After stabilising. Authors' judgement: Early transfer needs a medical team. Later surgery for scars can be planned.

**Care needed.** Burn unit care, skin grafting and release of scar contractures.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Burn centres, and plastic and reconstructive surgery for later scar repair.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Surgery and trauma 2%; Armed conflict: Intensive care 20%, Surgery and trauma 4%, Rehabilitation 4%.

### Crush injury with kidney failure

People trapped under rubble can develop kidney failure and need dialysis within days.

- Among 1,024 crush syndrome patients after the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, 68% developed acute kidney injury and 488 had dialysis (48%, calculated). [10]
- Crush syndrome was present in 128 of 201 adults (64%) admitted within two weeks of the earthquakes to 12 intensive care units. [11]

**Moving abroad.** After stabilising. After the 2023 earthquakes, 1,715 patients were moved by plane and helicopter to hospitals in other cities. The source does not separate crush injury. [12] Authors' judgement: Dialysis has to start close to the disaster, so transfer abroad is unlikely in the first days.

**Care needed.** Dialysis, intensive care, fasciotomy and wound care.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Intensive care and dialysis units.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Intensive care 64%, Dialysis 100%.

### Face and jaw injury

Blast injuries to the face need staged surgery and later dental prosthetic work.

- Facial injuries were 36 of the 858 injuries on the same WHO register (4.2%, calculated). No earthquake figure was found. [1]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. Authors' judgement: Planned surgery, usually weeks or months after injury.

**Care needed.** Jaw and facial reconstruction, then dental prosthetics.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Maxillofacial and plastic surgery. This is where dental clinics have a role.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Surgery and trauma 2%; Armed conflict: Surgery and trauma 4.2%.

### Eye injury and eye disease

Blast fragments and lost access to eye care both threaten sight.

- Ophthalmology was the fourth most common need in a WHO-supported medical evacuation programme, with 269 patients. [13]
- Eye surgery was 7% of war-related operations in the same ten-year series at a border hospital. [8]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. Authors' judgement: Planned surgery in most cases.

**Care needed.** Eye surgery and sight rehabilitation.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Eye hospitals and clinics that already treat international patients.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Surgery and trauma 1%; Armed conflict: Surgery and trauma 2%.

### Chest, abdomen and pelvis injury

Serious injuries to the trunk are treated urgently at home. Referrals abroad are for complications and later repair.

- Abdominal and pelvic injuries were 43 of the 858 injuries on the same WHO register (5%, calculated). [1]
- General surgery (9.5%) and thoracic surgery (5.2%) together made up 15% of war-related operations in the same ten-year series at a border hospital (sum calculated). [8]

**Moving abroad.** After stabilising. One national evacuation programme moves patients as soon as they are stable enough for transport. [6]

**Care needed.** General, thoracic and urological surgery.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** General and thoracic surgery in authorised hospitals.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Intensive care 16%, Surgery and trauma 12%, Rehabilitation 5.1%; Armed conflict: Intensive care 35%, Surgery and trauma 6.8%, Rehabilitation 13%.

### Critical illness in an epidemic

An epidemic can fill intensive care units, and few infectious patients have been moved between countries.

- In 2020 Germany accepted 44 patients from Italy, 130 from France and 46 from the Netherlands. [14]

**Moving abroad.** Partly. European Union countries moved patients across borders in 2020, in the numbers above. [14] Authors' judgement: Transfer abroad is likely to stay limited.

**Care needed.** Intensive care, ventilation and infection control.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Intensive care units of authorised hospitals.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Epidemic: Intensive care 100%.

### Nursing and recovery after hospital

Patients who leave hospital may still need wound care, nursing or a place to stay near treatment.

- In a rehabilitation unit in Ankara that treated Kahramanmaraş earthquake survivors, 15% of patients had pressure ulcers and 52% had abrasions or superficial wounds. [4]
- More than half of intensive care survivors develop post-intensive care syndrome. [15]
- About 100,000 earthquake survivors were hosted in hotels one week after the 2023 earthquakes. The report does not mention medical care. [16]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. Authors' judgement: Follows hospital discharge.

**Care needed.** Nursing, wound care and physiotherapy in a supported setting.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Hotels and thermal facilities with nursing support.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Sudden disaster: Step-down recovery 100%; Armed conflict: Step-down recovery 100%; Epidemic: Step-down recovery 100%.

## Treatment cut off by the crisis

### Cancer treatment

Cancer treatment stops when hospitals, drugs or radiotherapy are lost, and delay shortens survival.

- Oncology made up 37% of applications for permits to travel for treatment in one month of a WHO report on a restricted-access setting. [17]
- Cancer was the second most common need in a WHO-supported medical evacuation programme, with 759 patients. [13]
- In a systematic review, each four-week delay in cancer surgery was associated with a 6 to 8% higher risk of death. [18]
- Türkiye had 262 radiotherapy devices in 2019, against an estimated need of 332 to 411. [19]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. Authors' judgement: If fit to travel. Treatment lasts months, so patients need lodging near the hospital.

**Care needed.** Surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy over several months.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Oncology and radiotherapy centres, and hotels for patients on long courses.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Armed conflict: Cancer care 100%; Epidemic: Cancer care 100%.

### Long-term dialysis

People with kidney failure need dialysis several times a week, without a gap.

- In one armed conflict, 57% of dialysis patients surveyed could not keep regular sessions. [20]
- One conflict-affected area had about 1,100 people with kidney failure before the conflict. [21]

**Moving abroad.** Partly. Approvals for evacuating children with kidney failure took weeks to months in one documented conflict. [21] Authors' judgement: Possible only where dialysis continues without interruption.

**Care needed.** Regular dialysis with lodging nearby.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Dialysis centres, including those that serve travellers.

**Share of direct referrals in the model.** Armed conflict: Dialysis 100%; Epidemic: Dialysis 100%.

### Heart disease

Heart disease needs surgery and catheter services that crises interrupt.

- Cardiovascular disease was among the five most common needs in a WHO-supported medical evacuation programme, with 247 patients. [13]
- Cardiology was 9% of permit applications in the same WHO report. [17]
- Türkiye had 207 heart surgery centres in 46 provinces in about 2009. [22]

**Moving abroad.** After stabilising. Authors' judgement: Once stable enough for transport.

**Care needed.** Heart surgery, catheter procedures and follow-up.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Heart centres of authorised hospitals.

### Children with congenital disease

Children born with heart or other anomalies need surgery that cannot wait for long.

- Congenital anomalies were the third most common need in a WHO-supported medical evacuation programme, with 272 patients. [13]
- Türkiye had 22 congenital heart surgery centres in nine cities in about 2009. [22]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. Authors' judgement: With a parent or caregiver.

**Care needed.** Paediatric surgery and long follow-up.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** University and city hospitals with paediatric surgery.

### Blood disease

Thalassaemia, haemophilia and leukaemia need regular treatment and sometimes a marrow transplant.

- Haematology made up 11% of permit applications in the same WHO report. [17]
- A statement published in January 2014 gave 59 stem cell transplant centres registered with the Ministry of Health in Türkiye. [23]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. Authors' judgement: If fit to travel.

**Care needed.** Transfusions, factor treatment and marrow transplant.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** Haematology and marrow transplant centres.

## Routine care in a long crisis

### Ordinary treatment no longer available at home

When a crisis lasts for years, people also travel for everyday treatment. This part is health tourism in the usual sense.

- In one Turkish province, 3,637 of 15,907 foreign patients in 2011 to 2017 (22.8%) came from four countries that the study links to war. Oncology, orthopaedics and gynaecology came first, then dental and eye care. [24]
- One commentary reports that only 10 to 15% of patients sent abroad at state expense in one crisis had war-related injuries. [25]
- A commentary on patients leaving conflict-affected countries for care reports that many families sell belongings or borrow money to pay. [26]

**Moving abroad.** Can travel. Authors' judgement: Planned travel.

**Care needed.** Planned surgery, women's health, dental and eye care.

**Health tourism capacity that can help.** The usual health tourism services.

## Needs that stay at home

- **First hours of emergency care.** WHO notes that the most timely response to trauma comes from the affected country, and that complex foreign field teams are unlikely to be working for at least 5 to 7 days. [27] Authors' judgement: The first hours of care cannot wait for transfer abroad.
- **Long-term medicines for chronic disease.** A review of humanitarian settings describes care integrated into primary care as the ideal model, with referral mechanisms for conditions that cannot be managed there. [28]
- **Mental health.** Affects about six times more people than those wounded in a conflict. [28] Authors' judgement: Care is long-term and close to home, so the model leaves it out.
- **Childbirth and newborn care.** No study on transfer abroad was found. Authors' judgement: Time-critical and delivered locally.

Hair transplant, cosmetic surgery, obesity surgery and fertility treatment are not needs that a crisis creates, so they are left out. The surgeons, operating rooms and beds of clinics in these fields are still part of the capacity the model can call on.

## How the shares were set

Numbers are percentages of a care line's direct referrals.

- **Armed conflict, surgery and rehabilitation.** Limb injury 50, amputation 22, spinal cord 6, brain 5, burns 4, face 4.2 and eye 2, in percent of 858 injuries on a WHO register of patients needing medical evacuation from a conflict zone. The register counts 858 injuries in 683 people, and the model applies the shares to referred patients. [1] Chest, abdomen and pelvis take the rest.
- **Armed conflict, intensive care.** All shares are assumptions. No source gives this mix.
- **Earthquake, intensive care.** Crush syndrome 64, from 128 of 201 adult intensive care patients. Skull fracture, 5, stands in for brain injury, which the source does not report. [11] Limb injury 15 and other injuries 16 divide the rest. Injury categories overlap in the source, so its figure for limb fracture (36%) cannot be used directly.
- **Earthquake, surgery.** Amputation 10, between 7% of primary surgeries and 14% of procedures in two earthquake studies. [2], [29] Limb injury 72 is the remainder, close to the 66% of injury cases with fractures in one of these studies. Burns 2, face 2, eye 1, brain 1 and other injuries 12 are assumptions. No earthquake figure was found for face or eye injury.
- **Earthquake, rehabilitation.** Amputation 21 is the mean of 31% and 11%. Spinal cord injury 8.5 is the mean of 5% and 12%. Brain injury 6.4 comes from one unit. [4], [5] Other injuries 5.1 are an assumption, and limb injury 59 is the remainder. The spinal cord count in the multicentre abstract does not reconcile and needs checking.

## Points to check before citing

- Every statement here was read through automated fetching and should be compared with the original.
- Yağcı 2023 is a conference abstract. Its author list could not be read in full.
- The author list of Idrees 2025 could not be read in full on the page opened.
- Mendeş 2025 is an early online article without volume or pages.
- The WHO summary sheets on medical evacuations print no publisher or date. In the December 2025 sheet, the pairing of countries with counts is unclear, so the figure for Türkiye is not used.
- The Stimson and The Conversation pieces are commentaries. Their pages show no date.
